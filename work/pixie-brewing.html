§ 01 — The starting point A folk-surreal pile, not a brand yet.
Three drawings · zero rules
Lithuanian outsider-illustration energy

Most breweries arrive with a strategy deck and ask for a logo. Pixie arrived with the opposite — three illustrations from a Lithuanian artist that already had personality coming out of them, and no system to hold them together. A garage on roller skates with hops bursting out the back. A heraldic bird made of patterned architecture. A symmetrical heart with an eye in the middle and plants growing skyward.

They were striking, weird, ceremonial and slightly psychedelic. They were not yet a brand. They had imperfections, asymmetry, hidden symbols and a clearly-authored hand — the kind of thing that looks like it belongs to a culture, not a marketing department. The brief, when we stripped it back, was simple: keep all of that weirdness, but make it scale. Make it work on a 33ml can lid, a 30L KeyKeg, a beer-festival glass, an Instagram feed, a pub-window vinyl, and a pump-clip across the bar — without sanitising any of it.

Original 01

The garage on wheels.

Architecture, machinery, biology and absurdity in one object. Introduced eyes-inside-forms and tendril extensions.

Original 02

The heraldic bird.

Medieval-emblem energy filtered through folk illustration. Wings as patterned architecture, hops as ceremony.

Original 03

The heart with the eye.

Symmetry, an emblem-like front-on stance, and a recurring archetype set — eyes, growth, transformation, hybrids.

§ 02 — Building the language We didn't pick a logo. We pulled a system out of the drawings.
Five visual principles
The DNA we kept through everything

Our first call was not to push any of the three illustrations toward becoming the mark. They were too detailed, too asymmetric and too narrative-driven to survive at favicon size. Great illustrations are usually weak logos. So we treated the drawings as a quarry — and went looking for the DNA underneath. Five principles emerged, and every later decision on this project answers back to them.

The eye, always.

An eye appears in every drawing, sometimes obvious, sometimes hidden. We made it central. Eyes solve a retail problem too — they create an instant focal point in a crowded fridge.

Hops as spirit, not cone.

Across the work, hops, leaves, feathers and flowers melt into one organic structure. We didn't draw literal hop cones for the mark — we kept it as a "hop spirit" sitting somewhere between flower, talisman and creature.

Thick black linework.

Heavy outlines are the glue. They unify chaotic colour, survive screen-printing, embroidery, etched glass and embossed lids, and keep the whole world feeling handmade no matter where it ends up.

Flat, unblended colour.

No gradients, no airbrush, no shading. Colour sits in flat blocks inside the linework — the visual logic of stained glass, folk woodcuts and printmaking, not digital illustration.

Controlled imperfection.

Wobble in the lines, uneven spacing, hand-set type. We protected this through every redraw. Over-polish kills the emotional honesty that made these drawings work in the first place.

Pattern as connective tissue.

Small repeated motifs — micro hops, leaves, dots, stars, tendrils — became the fill texture across packaging. They tie hero artwork to background, so nothing on a can ever feels orphaned.

§ 03 — The mark A totem, not a badge.
Eye, hops, tendrils, drip
One symbol, all five principles

The mark needed to do the impossible — condense all that folk-surreal chaos into one symbol you could read at favicon size, scale to a wall mural, and embroider onto a beanie. We landed on a vertical totem. An eye at the heart, hops blooming above, tendrils curling out the sides, and a single drop falling from the bottom. Three of the five principles live inside it; the linework and flat colour carry the other two.

Symmetric enough to feel mythic. Simple enough to scale. Distinctive enough that nobody confuses it with another brewery. Because we resisted the urge to draw a literal hop cone, the mark reads as ceremonial — a brewer's totem — rather than another generic craft-beer pictogram.

§ 04 — System variations Three reductions of the same idea, for every surface.
Eye motif · Vertical totem · PX monogram
One language, three voices

A single mark, no matter how good, will never carry a whole brewery alone. So we drew variations — three reductions of the same logic, each engineered for surfaces the primary totem couldn't comfortably cover.

01 — Eye motif
02 — Vertical totem
03 — PX monogram

The eye motif isolates the focal point — it lives on pump clips, on tap badges, on the back of tees where you need recognition at three metres but don't have room for a full lockup. The vertical totem is the wrap variant — it strips into one colour for glass etching, embroidery and tax-stamp foil. The PX monogram handles small-spot duties: bottle-cap liner, KeyKeg neck-ring sticker, hangtag corner. Together they cover every contact point the brewery has with its drinkers, without ever feeling like a watered-down sub-brand.

§ 05 — The catalogue Each beer is its own creature.
Eight illustrated cans & growing
Same language · different protagonist

Once the principles were locked, we kept commissioning new illustrations in the same hand for each beer Pixie released — a steadily-growing illustrated catalogue that turns the brewery into a kind of folk bestiary. Hop-haired pixie blowing a pipe of smoke for the Norsk Pilsner. A green crocodile carrying barrels for the Longest Green. A masked imperial-stout figure in a stained-glass robe. A heraldic bird for the red IPA. We treat each can less like a packaging job and more like a new character joining the troupe.

§ 06 — Hero & field We invented one rule that stops every can from screaming.
Hero anchor · creature characters · micro pattern
Why nothing looks like collage

The collapse trap with illustrative brands is that every element competes equally. Six bright shapes on a 33cl can with no hierarchy looks like a sticker album, not a packaging system. So we built one composition rule and applied it everywhere: hero and field. A single hero illustration anchors the centre. One or two secondary creatures sit in supporting positions. Around them, a quiet micro-pattern — small hops, tendrils, hidden eyes, leaves — becomes connective tissue rather than competition. The eye lands on the hero, wanders to the side characters, then drifts through the pattern. The can stops shouting and starts telling a story.

The Incognito Imperial Stout artwork is the rule at its most uncompromising. A single masked figure dressed in stained-glass robes anchors dead-centre. Hop plumes grow out either shoulder as secondary characters. The background fills with woven pattern, hidden eyes and curling tendrils — never bright enough to compete, never empty enough to leave the figure floating. You can read the can at three metres across a bar (mask + colour), at one metre as you reach for it (figure + hops), and at twenty centimetres in your hand (the hidden pattern).

Every Pixie can after this one follows the same hierarchy. The protagonist changes; the rule doesn't.

§ 07 — Colour & SKU strategy Black linework stays. Colour does the sorting.
Style families, not arbitrary brights
Recognition before the eye reads the name

Once the brewery grew past four beers, colour had to start earning its keep. So we made a structural decision: linework stays black across everything, and colour gets assigned by style family. Now a drinker can pick the right can off the shelf without reading a single word.

Family 01

Green / yellow — IPA & pale.

The brewery's home territory. Greens, hop yellows, soft chartreuse. Garage Daze, First Krush, Longest Green, On A Call all live here.

Family 02

Warm reds — red & amber.

Saturated reds, ember oranges. For Rib Ride and amber styles where toasty malt sits behind the hop. Reads warm against pub-low light.

Family 03

Blue / aqua — lagers.

Cooler blues and aqua greens for pilsners and clean lagers. Norsk Pilsner, Snap Crackle & Hop. Crisp on shelf next to the warmer IPAs.

Family 04

Deep earth — stout & dark.

Muted brown, plum, indigo and ink. Incognito Imperial Stout, Get Stuffed Christmas Stout, Discovery brown. Heavier — and visually it should look heavier too.

The system is deliberately loose. Inside each family there's room for the illustration to play — a Rib Ride bird can be more orange than red, a Norsk pixie can be all green with a single blue accent. What stays consistent is the family the can belongs to, so the brewery can scale to thirty SKUs and still read as one shelf at a beer festival.

The line that ran through everything

Strange beers. Strange friends. Good times.

§ 08 — The website A site you fall into, not a SaaS template.
brand.pixiebrewing.com
Single page · custom WebGL hero · world bible

The site brief came out of one sentence we wrote into the project's house rules: build a world, not pages. Every other craft-brewery site we looked at felt like a content-managed marketing template that happened to be about beer. We wanted the opposite — a site that felt like stepping into the brewery's mythology, where every section was discovered rather than navigated to.

01 — Homepage

Cinematic hero, shader-driven.

The hero isn't a video tag. We bound a 4K footage loop to a custom GLSL shader on a canvas — grain, paper texture and a slow drift live on top of it in real time, so the brewery world starts moving before anything reads "brewery".

WebGL · GLSL · 460vh sticky scroll
02 — Brand bible

A second page just for the world.

brand.html is its own atmospheric long-scroll — manifesto, palette, voice, the strange-beers catalogue, the marks, the milestones. It's the brewery's mythology document made into a website.

Long-scroll · paper-texture sections
03 — Merch storefront

Print-on-demand in an overlay.

Rather than break the cinematic single-universe feel, the merch store opens as a full-screen overlay. Products pull live from the Brandflow POD platform; checkout happens inside the overlay's iframe so the user never leaves the world.

Brandflow POD API · iframe checkout overlay

Under the hood, the site is deliberately old-school: three hand-written HTML files, no framework, no build step. Inline CSS, inline JS, custom WebGL where it earns it. We made that call early — a brewery this hand-drawn deserves a hand-built site. A React/Next stack would have added speed for the dev, slowness for the visitor, and a layer of template-feel that nothing else in the project has.
